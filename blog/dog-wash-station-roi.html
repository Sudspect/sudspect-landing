Dog Wash Station ROI: What Florida Properties Are Seeing After 12 Months

Occupancy lift, resident satisfaction scores, and ancillary income — here is what the numbers look like one year after installing a self-service dog wash vending machine.

When a property manager hears "dog wash vending machine," the first question is almost always the same: what is the actual return? It is a fair question. Amenity decisions compete for budget, board approval, and physical space. A good-looking amenity that does not move the needle on retention or income is just noise.

After working with apartment communities and HOAs across Florida, we have started to see consistent patterns in what properties gain after 12 months with a self-service dog wash station in place. Here is what the data and feedback look like.

The Baseline: What Florida Properties Look Like Before Installation

Most Florida multifamily properties we work with have some form of pet policy — pet deposits, pet fees, and in some cases pet rent. What they rarely have is a dedicated amenity that serves pet-owning residents rather than just charging them.

The average property in the 150- to 400-unit range we work with has between 25% and 45% of residents with at least one dog. That is a significant share of your population with an unmet need: a clean, convenient place to bathe their dog without making a mess of their unit, hauling the dog to a groomer, or fighting with a hose in the parking lot.

The dog wash station fills that gap. The ROI question is really about how much that gap was costing you — in resident dissatisfaction, unit turnover, and maintenance call volume — before it was filled.

Occupancy and Lease Renewal Impact

The most consistent finding across Florida properties is the effect on lease renewal conversations. Pet-owning residents frequently cite pet amenities as a deciding factor when choosing to renew — or not. When a property has nothing meaningful to offer pet owners beyond a grassy patch, the renewal pitch is weak.

Properties with a dog wash station in place for 12 months report that it becomes one of the top three amenities mentioned by pet-owning residents in renewal surveys. It is not a minor perk — for dog owners, it is a utility. They use it regularly, and they notice when they move somewhere that does not have it.

The downstream effect: communities with dog wash stations see measurably lower pet-owner turnover compared to their own prior-year numbers. Replacing a resident costs a property an average of one to two months of rent in vacancy, concessions, and turnover prep. If the station retains even two additional pet-owning households per year, it more than justifies its existence on retention alone.

Revenue Share: What Properties Are Actually Earning

Under Sudspect's model, the property earns a revenue share from every wash session — with no capital outlay, no maintenance responsibilities, and no staffing required. The machine handles everything; Sudspect handles restocking and service.

Revenue share income depends on the property's pet density and how frequently residents use the station. At a typical Florida apartment community with 200 units and 35% pet penetration, a conservative usage estimate of two washes per pet-owning household per month generates meaningful ancillary income over 12 months. Higher pet density properties — especially those in suburban Tampa, Orlando, and South Florida where families with large dogs are common — tend to see higher utilization.

This income does not require the property to do anything differently. It arrives because the machine is placed, maintained, and operational. For HOA communities, that revenue flows to the association budget and can offset dues increases or fund other improvements.

Maintenance Call Reduction

One ROI category that often surprises property managers is the drop in pet-related maintenance calls. Before a dog wash station, residents bathe their dogs in bathtubs and showers. The result: clogged drains, damaged tub surrounds, and housekeeping complaints.

Properties report a noticeable reduction in pet-related drain service calls and unit cleaning costs tied to pet odor after the dog wash station has been in place for six or more months. Residents who have a clean, dedicated space to use simply use it — especially when it is more convenient and less messy than doing it at home.

The machine runs a full disinfection cycle between every wash, which also prevents pet odor from building up in common areas. Maintenance teams report fewer odor-related complaints from the amenity rooms where machines are installed compared to the general hallway and lobby complaints they fielded before.

Resident Satisfaction Scores

Properties using resident satisfaction platforms — whether formal survey tools or Google reviews — consistently see pet amenities mentioned positively within the first 90 days of installation. Dog-owning residents leave reviews specifically calling out the dog wash station as a differentiating feature, which helps properties attract new pet-owning applicants organically through search.

A Florida property with 300 units saw its average Google rating improve by a measurable margin in the six months after installation, with multiple reviews directly mentioning the dog wash station. For a property trying to compete in a crowded market, that organic visibility is real value.

The 12-Month Picture for Property Managers

After 12 months, the typical Florida property with a Sudspect dog wash station in place has seen three measurable outcomes: a reduction in pet-owner turnover, a new monthly revenue stream from the machine, and fewer maintenance calls tied to in-unit pet bathing. None of these required upfront investment from the property.

For HOA communities, the value shows up differently — in resident satisfaction at annual meetings, in the ability to point to a tangible amenity improvement, and in revenue that reduces the pressure on annual dues.

The ROI case is not complicated. A dog wash station that costs the property nothing to install and nothing to maintain, pays the property a share of every wash, reduces turnover, and improves resident satisfaction is not a hard sell to a board or ownership group. The question is usually just timing.
