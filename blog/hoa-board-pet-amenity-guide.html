What Florida HOA Boards Need to Know Before Adding a Pet Amenity

Pet amenities are increasingly on the HOA agenda — but boards need to weigh liability, budget impact, maintenance, and resident demand before saying yes. Here's the complete evaluation framework.

Pet amenities have moved from a nice-to-have to a board-level conversation in Florida HOA communities. With pet ownership at record highs and residents increasingly treating their dogs as family members, the pressure on boards to provide pet-specific infrastructure has grown considerably. Dog parks, pet waste stations, and self-service dog wash stations are all showing up in resident surveys, board meetings, and capital improvement discussions.

But HOA boards have a fiduciary responsibility to evaluate any new amenity carefully. The resident demand may be real — but what are the actual costs, risks, and operational implications? This guide walks through the questions every Florida HOA board should ask before approving a pet amenity, and how modern programs have answered many of them.

1. What Does It Actually Cost the Association?

This is always the first question, and rightfully so. HOA boards are stewards of the community's reserve fund and operating budget. Any new amenity has to be evaluated for both upfront capital cost and ongoing operating expense.

Traditional pet amenities — dog parks, agility equipment, pet washing areas with plumbing — can run $10,000 to $50,000 or more to install, plus ongoing maintenance, insurance coverage, and eventual replacement costs. These are legitimate budget items, but they require reserve fund access or special assessment approval.

Newer revenue-share amenity programs have changed this calculus. Self-service dog wash stations from providers like Sudspect are installed at zero cost to the HOA. The provider handles installation, maintenance, and equipment replacement. The association pays nothing upfront and nothing ongoing — and earns a revenue share from usage fees paid directly by residents. For boards managing tight budgets, this model is particularly compelling because it converts a cost line into an income line.

2. Who Handles Maintenance and Liability?

HOA boards are rightly cautious about amenities that create new liability exposure. A dog park, for example, can create slip-and-fall claims, dog bite incidents between residents, and ongoing maintenance obligations the board isn't equipped to manage.

Self-service enclosed dog wash stations reduce many of these risks. Usage is individual — one resident and one dog at a time — eliminating dog-to-dog interaction incidents. The machine itself is maintained by the program provider, not the association. Boards should still review any provider's liability language and confirm that their property insurance covers the footprint where the machine is installed, but the risk profile is meaningfully lower than open-play pet amenities.

For the water and drain connections, a licensed plumber handles installation, and the property's water supply is used — a minor cost that gets built into the resident pricing so there's no separate utility invoice for the HOA.

3. Is There Genuine Resident Demand?

Before approving any amenity, a board should have a reasonable sense of whether residents actually want it. For pet amenities, a simple survey distributed via email or community management software can answer the question quickly. Key data points: how many households have dogs, how often they currently bathe their dogs, and whether they'd use an on-site option.

In most Florida HOA communities of 150+ homes, the answer is consistent: 30 to 50 percent of households have at least one dog, and a significant share of those residents are currently making off-site trips to pet stores or groomers for basic bathing. That's unmet demand that an on-site station can capture immediately.

4. What Does the Approval Process Look Like Under Florida Law?

Florida HOA governance is governed by Chapter 720 of the Florida Statutes. Most amenity additions require board approval and may require a vote of the full membership depending on the community's governing documents and whether the addition involves a material alteration to common elements.

For zero-cost revenue-share programs where no capital outlay is required and no structural modification to the common area is made beyond a utility connection, the approval threshold is often lower — typically a board majority vote rather than a membership vote. Boards should review their governing documents and consult with their community association attorney, particularly for any permanent fixture installation.

5. How Does It Affect Property Values?

Pet-friendly amenities have a measurable positive effect on property values and desirability in Florida communities. Real estate data consistently shows that pet-friendly features — particularly purpose-built infrastructure rather than just a policy — command premium pricing in both sales and rentals. For HOA communities where resale value is a key concern for members, adding a visible, well-maintained pet amenity supports the overall value proposition of the community.

A self-service dog wash station is also a differentiator in community marketing. Many Florida HOAs compete for new residents against nearby developments. An on-site dog wash station that's visible in listing photos and community tours is a tangible feature that prospective buyers and renters with pets will notice — and remember.

6. What Are the Right Questions to Ask Any Provider?

When evaluating a specific self-service dog wash station program, HOA boards should ask: Who pays for installation? Who handles maintenance and repairs? What happens if the machine breaks down? How are supplies managed? What is the revenue share percentage and how is it calculated? What is the contract term and what are the exit provisions?

A reputable provider will have clear, written answers to all of these. Red flags include vague maintenance commitments, unusually long contract terms with no exit provisions, or revenue share language that guarantees specific income numbers rather than a percentage of actual usage.
