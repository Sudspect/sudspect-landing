How to Pitch a Dog Wash Station to Your HOA Board: A Property Manager's Playbook

Board members ask three questions about any new amenity: what does it cost, who is responsible for it, and what problem does it solve? Here's how to answer all three — and get your vote.

HOA boards are not hard to convince when you speak their language. They are not opposed to good ideas — they are opposed to cost, liability, and maintenance headaches. If you walk into that meeting with answers to all three objections built into your opening statement, you will have a much easier conversation than you expect.

This is the playbook Florida property managers use to get a dog wash station approved — sometimes in a single board meeting.

Start With the Problem, Not the Solution

Never open a board presentation by describing the machine. Open by describing the problem the machine solves.

At most Florida communities with 150+ units and a pet policy, a meaningful percentage of residents are bathing dogs in their unit tubs. That behavior damages p-traps and drains, creates odor complaints from adjacent units, and leads to grooming service costs that residents inevitably blame on the property. Pet-related damage complaints are one of the top five maintenance tickets at multifamily communities in Florida.

Frame it this way: "We currently have no designated space for pet grooming, and residents are improvising in their units. That's creating maintenance tickets and neighbor complaints we can reduce with a simple solution." Board members respond to problems they recognize — and every board has heard these complaints.

Lead With Zero Cost to the Association

The single most important fact in your pitch is that a revenue-share dog wash station installation carries no capital cost to the HOA or community. The vendor installs the machine, handles all maintenance and repairs, and splits revenue with the property from day one.

Say it plainly and early: "This is a zero-cost installation. The association puts no money in. We receive a monthly revenue share from day one." Then stop talking and let that land. Board members are waiting for the catch — and there isn't one. Give them a moment to absorb that.

You should also clarify the maintenance agreement: the vendor owns the machine and services it. If it breaks, the vendor fixes it. The property manager's role is essentially zero after installation. This eliminates the "who has to deal with this?" objection before it's asked.

Address the Space Question Directly

Boards often assume a dog wash station requires significant dedicated square footage. The reality is an automated unit needs approximately 80 square feet — the footprint of a large bathroom. Most communities have a laundry room, storage corridor, or utility space that can accommodate this without impacting any other community function.

Come to the meeting with a specific location in mind. "I've identified a 90-square-foot space adjacent to the ground-floor laundry room that isn't currently used for anything else." A concrete proposal is always stronger than a hypothetical one. Boards approve specific plans; they table vague concepts.

Answer the Revenue Question With Real Numbers

Board members will ask — or should ask — what the financial return looks like. Be ready with conservative estimates based on your community's size and pet adoption rate.

A standard revenue-share model: washes priced at $24.99–$39.99 per wash. At a 200-unit community with 35% pet adoption (70 pet-owning households) and an average of two washes per month per household, gross monthly revenue is approximately $2,100–$2,400. The property's share depends on the specific agreement, but revenue-share arrangements typically deliver $400–$800 per month to the community as passive income.

Present a conservative scenario, not a best-case scenario. Boards are skeptical of optimistic projections. If your conservative estimate still shows positive return with zero capital invested, the math makes itself.

Prepare for the Top Three Objections

Objection 1: "What if residents damage it?" The machine is vendor-owned and vendor-maintained. Vandalism or misuse is the vendor's problem under the service agreement, not the association's. Your answer: "We have no financial exposure — that's the vendor's liability."

Objection 2: "What about water usage and utility costs?" A typical self-service wash cycle uses approximately 10–12 gallons of water — comparable to a residential shower. Monthly utility impact for a moderately used machine is $30–$60 in water and electricity. This is disclosed in the installation agreement and factored into the revenue share arrangement.

Objection 3: "Does this change our pet policy or create new rules we have to enforce?" No. The machine is a shared amenity with the same access rules as the laundry room or fitness center. Residents swipe their key fob or pay at the machine. No staff oversight required. No new policy needed beyond adding the amenity to the community's common-area list.

Close With a Specific Next Step

End your presentation with a clear, low-commitment ask. "I'd like authorization to schedule a site evaluation and review the installation agreement. There's no obligation at that stage — we're just gathering the details to bring back a formal proposal." This moves the decision from "yes or no to the amenity" to "yes or no to learning more." The second question is almost always easier to get approved.

Once the vendor has done a site visit and presented the specific agreement, the board's final decision is much simpler: they have all the numbers, the proposed space, the maintenance terms, and the revenue split in writing. Approval at that stage is routine.
