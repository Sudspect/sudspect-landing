Pet Deposit vs Pet Fee vs Pet Rent: What Florida Landlords Should Know

Most Florida property managers use these three terms interchangeably — but they carry very different legal and financial implications. Here's how to structure your pet policy the right way.

Florida's pet-owning renter population is growing fast. More than 67% of U.S. households own a pet, and renters with pets are actively seeking communities that accommodate them. For property managers and HOA directors, that demand creates both opportunity and legal responsibility.

One area where many communities trip up: the difference between a pet deposit, a pet fee, and pet rent. Using the wrong term — or the wrong structure — can leave you under-compensated for real damage, or worse, expose you to legal challenges under Florida law.

What Is a Pet Deposit?

A pet deposit is a refundable sum collected upfront to cover potential damage caused by a pet. Under Florida Statute 83.43, security deposits — including pet deposits — must follow specific rules around holding, accounting, and return timelines (typically within 15–30 days of move-out depending on whether deductions are made).

Because pet deposits are refundable, they offer limited protection if actual damage exceeds the deposit amount. They also require more administrative work: tracking the funds, documenting condition at move-in and move-out, and processing returns or deduction claims.

Best for: Communities that want to hold residents accountable for specific pet-caused damage while maintaining a fair, refundable structure.

What Is a Pet Fee?

A pet fee is a one-time, non-refundable charge paid at the start of a lease. Unlike a deposit, it doesn't need to be returned — but for that same reason, it cannot be applied toward actual damages at move-out. It simply offsets the administrative cost of allowing pets on the property.

Florida law does not cap one-time pet fees, giving landlords flexibility. However, because it's non-refundable and not tied to damage, some residents push back on fees they view as punitive rather than purposeful.

Best for: Properties that want a simple, low-overhead charge to offset baseline pet-related costs (extra cleaning, wear on common areas) without managing a deposit escrow.

What Is Pet Rent?

Pet rent is a recurring monthly charge added to the base rent specifically because a tenant has a pet. It is not refundable and is not applied to damage. Instead, it functions as ongoing compensation for the incremental wear, maintenance, and amenity costs that pet-owning residents generate over time.

Pet rent is increasingly popular in multifamily communities because it creates a predictable, recurring revenue stream that scales with the number of pet-owning residents. In South Florida markets, pet rent commonly ranges from $25 to $100 per month depending on property class and pet size.

Best for: Apartment communities and HOAs looking to generate consistent ancillary income from their pet policy rather than one-time or refundable charges.

Can You Charge All Three?

Yes — and many Florida properties do. A common structure is: a one-time non-refundable pet fee at move-in, a refundable pet deposit to cover potential damage, and monthly pet rent for the duration of the lease. Florida law does not prohibit stacking these charges, provided the lease is transparent about each one and their respective terms.

The key is clear documentation. Each charge should be listed separately in the lease with explicit language about whether it is refundable, what it covers, and how disputes are resolved.

What About Service Animals and ESAs?

Under the Fair Housing Act, properties cannot charge pet fees, pet deposits, or pet rent for tenants with documented service animals or emotional support animals (ESAs). This applies even if the property has a strict no-pet policy. Florida law aligns with federal FHA requirements in this area.

Property managers should have a documented ESA request and verification process in place. Requiring reasonable documentation (a letter from a licensed healthcare provider, for example) is permissible — but demanding veterinary records or breed verification for ESAs is not.

Key Takeaways for Florida Property Managers

  • Pet deposits are refundable and governed by Florida's security deposit statutes — track them carefully.
  • Pet fees are non-refundable one-time charges — simple, but limited in what they cover.
  • Pet rent is recurring monthly income — the best structure for long-term revenue from pet-owning residents.
  • You can legally combine all three — just document each clearly in the lease.
  • Service animals and ESAs are exempt from all pet charges under the Fair Housing Act.
  • Amenities like on-site dog wash stations reduce pet-related complaints and strengthen your pet-friendly positioning.
