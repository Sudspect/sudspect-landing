How Revenue-Share Amenities Are Changing the HOA Budget Conversation

For decades, HOA amenities were a cost center. Revenue-share programs are flipping that model — letting communities add value for residents while generating income. Here's what Florida HOA boards need to know.

The HOA budget conversation has traditionally gone one direction: costs go up, dues go up. Insurance premiums rise, maintenance contracts inflate, reserve studies come back with uncomfortable numbers, and the board has to decide how much of that to pass along to homeowners. It's a conversation most board members and community managers dread — and residents resent.

Revenue-share amenity programs are quietly changing that dynamic. Instead of every new amenity requiring a line item in the budget, some amenities now pay the community back. It's a model that's gaining traction in Florida's multifamily and HOA market, and it's worth understanding what it is, how it works, and where the real opportunities are.

What Is a Revenue-Share Amenity Program?

A revenue-share amenity program is an arrangement where a third-party vendor installs and operates an amenity on your property — at no cost to the HOA or apartment community — and splits the revenue generated by resident usage with the property.

The model is most common in amenities that residents pay to use directly: vending machines, EV charging stations, package lockers, laundry facilities, and increasingly, self-service pet care stations. The vendor owns and maintains the equipment. The property provides the space and, ideally, helps drive awareness among residents. Usage revenue is split according to a pre-agreed percentage.

From the HOA's perspective, this looks like: zero capital outlay, zero ongoing maintenance responsibility, a new amenity residents actually use, and a monthly check.

Why This Model Is Growing in Florida

Florida HOAs face a specific set of pressures that make revenue-share programs especially appealing right now. Property insurance costs have surged dramatically over the past several years, putting pressure on operating budgets that were already thin. Meanwhile, residents — particularly younger homeowners and renters — expect more from their communities in terms of lifestyle amenities.

The result is a classic squeeze: boards need to add value to attract and retain residents while simultaneously managing costs. Revenue-share amenities break out of that squeeze by adding a resident-facing service that also generates income. It doesn't solve the insurance problem, but it moves the budget conversation in a different direction.

How the Numbers Work

Revenue-share percentages vary by vendor and amenity type, but the math is straightforward. Take a self-service dog wash station as an example. Sessions are priced in the $28–$35 range. In a community of 200+ homes with meaningful pet ownership — which describes most Florida HOA communities — even modest utilization generates real monthly income.

At 50 sessions per month at $30 average, that's $1,500 in gross revenue. A 20% revenue share to the community is $300/month, or $3,600/year — without the HOA spending a dollar on equipment, installation, or upkeep. As utilization grows (which it typically does once residents discover the station and tell their neighbors), so does the community's share.

That's not a budget-transforming number on its own, but it's real income that didn't exist before. And it compounds: the same community might also have a revenue-share arrangement with an EV charging provider and a package locker operator. Stacked together, these arrangements start to add up to something meaningful on the balance sheet.

What HOA Boards Need to Evaluate

Not all revenue-share programs are created equal. Before entering any agreement, HOA boards should evaluate:

  • Who owns the equipment? The vendor should own and be fully responsible for the machine. The HOA should not be on the hook for repairs, replacements, or end-of-life costs.
  • What are the contract terms? Length of agreement, termination rights, and what happens if the vendor goes out of business are all worth understanding before signing.
  • What space and utilities are required? Most equipment needs power (standard 120V in the case of dog wash stations) and a designated location. Confirm the physical requirements before committing.
  • How is revenue tracked and paid? Transparent reporting and predictable payment schedules matter. Ask to see sample statements and understand how disputes are handled.
  • Does it fit the community's demographics? A dog wash station makes sense for a community with high pet ownership. An EV charging station makes sense for a community with high EV adoption. Match the amenity to the actual resident profile.

The Resident Experience Benefit

Revenue is only part of the story. The other half is what these amenities do for resident satisfaction and retention. An HOA that adds a new service residents genuinely want — without raising dues — earns significant goodwill. That goodwill translates to lower turnover, more cooperative residents, and a community reputation that helps attract quality new homeowners when properties come to market.

For Florida communities competing for residents who have real choices about where to live, that reputation matters. A community known for thoughtful, well-maintained amenities that actually benefit daily life commands better prices and attracts better-fit residents than one that just has the basics.

Getting Started

How many drive EVs? What services are they currently leaving the community to access that you could bring on-site?
